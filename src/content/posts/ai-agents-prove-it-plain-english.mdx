AI coding agents are confident. They say *"this change is safe"* and *"this is
faster"* in the same tone whether it's true or not. Most of the time they're
right. When they're wrong, the mistake often passes every test and shows up
later as money in the wrong place.

This post explains a project I've been running, in plain words: what I tried,
what I found, and where I think it's worth using.

## The goal

**Make an AI agent prove its claims before anyone believes them.**

Not "the tests pass", but evidence anyone can check: a mathematical proof that
the change keeps the rules, a concrete example where it breaks them, or a
careful timing measurement.

## The idea in one paragraph

Every "this change is safe and faster" claim is really two claims. *Does it
still give the right answers?* That can be checked, with certainty, by a proof
checker (I used one called Lean). *Is it faster?* That can only be measured,
and measurements are noisy. So the right answer is checked first. Nothing gets
timed until it passes, and a fast change that gives wrong answers is rejected,
however fast it is.

## What I tested it on

Two test subjects, chosen to be harder each time:

1. **Part 1: a small billing system an AI agent built.** It handles bills,
   payments, payments made in advance, and reversals (undoing an entry). The agent then proposed six changes to make
   it faster.
2. **Part 2: someone else's design.** Precise Ledger Pro is an open-source loan
   ledger with a research paper. The paper describes a shortcut for fixing the
   books when a transaction arrives late, instead of recalculating everything
   from the start. I didn't write the code, and neither did the agent.

## What I did

For each one:

1. **Wrote down the rules** the system must always keep, such as "a payment is
   never applied for more than its amount" and "one customer's actions never change another
   customer's balance". The agent drafted them. **I reviewed and locked them**,
   so nobody (including the agent) can quietly change them afterwards.
2. **Checked each claim against those rules** with the proof checker. The
   result is a proof that the claim always holds, or a short, concrete case
   where it fails.
3. **Ran the failing cases through the real code**, to confirm the problem is in
   the software and not just in my write-up of it.
4. **Timed only what passed**, with a strict rule. It counts as faster only if
   its slowest run beat the old version's fastest run.

## What I found

**Part 1, the agent's billing system:**

- **4 of the 6 speed-ups were safe.** Together they made the system **5.03×
  faster**.
- **2 were rejected, and both look fine in everyday use:**
  - *"Only look at the first 20 unpaid bills."* It breaks only once a customer
    has 21: a payment then leaves money unused while a bill stays unpaid. The
    checker produced the exact case.
  - *"Use the faster disk setting."* Every test passed. But that setting can lose
    a payment the system has already confirmed if the power cuts out, and the
    agent never mentioned that trade-off. The checker caught it by reading the
    live database's settings.
- **The checker couldn't be fooled.** We tried 13 ways to fake a proof, and all
  13 were rejected.
- **My review mattered as much as the proofs.** The agent's first rules could
  be proved true, and three of them were still about the wrong thing. For example,
  "oldest bill first" meant the bill *created* first, not the one with the
  earliest bill date. No proof
  can catch a rule that's correctly proved but means the wrong thing; a person
  has to read it.

**Part 2, someone else's published design:**

- **The paper's shortcut usually agrees with a full recalculation, but not
  always.** I found three short sequences of transactions where it ends with
  the total split differently, for example between principal and excess. Two
  of them happen in the project's own code. The third disagrees only with a
  rule I chose for same-day ties, which the paper doesn't promise.
- **Four transactions are enough to make the shortcut not finish** in the
  project's two implementations, and in my model of the paper. That isn't proof
  it never finishes, but none of the three ever did. The paper already flags
  this area as open work.
- **A proved-correct version of the same idea, which could help fix it.**
  Like the paper's shortcut, it starts from the late transaction's date and
  recalculates only what follows. With a few changes in how it starts and how it
  handles reversals, it's **proved to give exactly the same answer** as
  recalculating everything. On the project's own code it was faster in all 32
  test workloads: **11.86× at the median**, between 1.96× and 155.94×.

## The conclusion

- **It catches the dangerous kind of wrong claim:** the one that looks fine in
  everyday use and in the usual tests. Both rejected speed-ups in part 1 were of
  that kind.
- **It works on code the agent didn't write.** It found real edge cases in a
  published design, and a proved-correct way to recalculate that could help
  fix the original method.
- **It doesn't replace a person.** Someone has to read and approve the rules.
  In both parts, my review and outside reviews caught mistakes the proofs
  couldn't.

## What it costs, and how efficient the skill is

I've packaged the method as two Claude Code skills and a reviewer agent, in the
public repo
[burden-of-proof](https://github.com/SuvrtSharma/burden-of-proof).
**I haven't measured how efficient they are yet**, in tokens, time, or how
often an agent gets the verdict right. That's the next test, and until then
I won't guess. Here is what I do know about the cost:

- **Proofs are much longer than the rules they prove.** Part 1's rules are 290
  lines, and the proofs are 1,400 lines. Part 2's rules are 463 lines, and the
  proofs are 671. An agent writes these, with many failed attempts along the way.
- **Checking a finished proof is cheap and automatic.** It takes seconds to
  minutes, with no tokens: the checker is an ordinary program that runs in CI.
- **Human review time is the real cost.** The rules have to be read by someone
  who knows the business.

The next test will give a fresh agent a set of claims with known answers (some
true, some false, some deliberately tricky) and count the tokens, the time and
the verdicts. The one number that has to be zero is false "verified" results.

## Where it's worth using

My judgement, not a measurement. It's worth the tokens when **a wrong answer
costs money, trust or compliance, and wouldn't show up in ordinary tests**. In
practice that means claims like "the new version gives the same answer as the
old one" or "this rule always holds" in:

- **Billing, payments, payroll, tax:** "this refactor gives the same totals";
  "a payment is never applied twice".
- **Stock, bookings, seat allocation:** "we can never oversell"; "releasing a
  hold restores the count exactly".
- **Permissions in multi-customer software:** "one customer can never see or
  change another's data".
- **Systems that replay history:** "a late or out-of-order event gives the same
  result as an on-time one".
- **Caches and running totals:** "the cached figure always equals the real one".
- **Migrations and rewrites:** "the new service returns what the old one did,
  for every input".
- **Performance changes:** "this is faster", measured properly, even when no
  proof is needed.

**Where it isn't worth it:** user interfaces, copy changes, simple
create-read-update screens, one-off scripts, and anything where nobody can
write down what "correct" means.

**Where it fits in a team's workflow:**

- **As a review step** on changes that touch money, permissions or history.
- **As a CI check.** The checker gives a plain pass or fail, so a pipeline can
  block a change whose proof doesn't check.
- **Before a migration cutover**, to show the new system gives the old system's
  answers.
- **Whenever an agent proposes an optimisation.** Check that it's right first,
  then time it.
