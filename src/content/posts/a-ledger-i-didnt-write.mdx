In [part 1](/blog/making-ai-agents-prove-their-claims) an AI agent built a small
ledger, proposed six optimisations, and a checker judged each one: Lean 4 proofs
for what it means, a benchmark for how fast it is, in that order. It worked, but
the agent had written both the code and the rules it was judged by. That's a
closed loop.

So part 2 needed a ledger nobody on this project wrote. I picked one I had a
reason to read closely: **Precise Ledger Pro**, a loan ledger with a Java
service, a Haskell port, and a draft paper describing how it corrects the ledger
when events arrive late. It was written by a mentor of mine.

What made it a good target is the paper itself. It is candid about where it is
unfinished. Its future-work section names, as the first open direction, *"a
formal proof of convergence and correctness for recursively nested retroactive
events"*. The second is benchmarking on synthetic ledgers. Those are exactly the
two things this toolkit is for. So that's what I set out to do. The first
findings went to the author before anything else, as failing tests in a pull
request.

## The problem the paper takes on

A loan ledger records disbursals, fees and payments, each with the date it takes
effect. The trouble is that events don't always arrive in that order. A payment
made on Monday is recorded on Thursday. A disbursal turns out to be a mistake and
is reversed a week later. Every balance and every allocation after that date may
now be wrong.

The safe answer is a **full rebuild**: re-apply every event in date order from
the start. It is correct by construction, and it gets slower as the ledger grows.
The paper's answer is **fork-and-sync**: copy the ledger at the late event's
date, replay what follows on the copy, and post individually traced adjustments
for any difference. It is a real engineering idea with sensible goals: correct
balances, an untouched audit trail, every correction traced to its cause, and
support for further corrections later. And the paper specifies it as clear,
step-by-step pseudocode, which is what made checking it possible at all.

In this project's terms, fork-and-sync is an **optimisation claim**: it promises
the full rebuild's answer at a lower cost. Part 1's rule applies. Check the
meaning first, then measure the speed.

## Part 1: rules from outside the code

The first step was deliberately *not* to read the code. I wrote down what a
production ledger has to do, from published practice and regulation, and turned
those rules into a model in Lean 4. The model has two halves: the full rebuild,
which is the definition of "right", and fork-and-sync, following the paper's
pseudocode step by step.

Every place where the pseudocode left a choice open became a named, written-down
reading. Then a person (me, as the owner) reviewed the model and locked it. The
lock is a set of SHA-256 fingerprints. Change one character and every proof is
rejected until the model is reviewed again. A proof only counts once the checker
accepts it against that lock, with two independent kernels and a test that the
proof's assumptions can actually be met.

Against the lock, **7 proofs are accepted**: some that the rebuild behaves well,
some that fork-and-sync doesn't always match it, and two about an optimisation
I'll come to.

## Part 2: what the model found

On short sequences of events, the model of fork-and-sync and the full rebuild
mostly agree, but not always. The checker accepts three proofs of disagreement,
each a concrete counterexample. The simplest has three events: a disbursal of
10 dated day 2, then two that arrive after it but are dated earlier, a disbursal
of 10 on day 0 and a payment of 20 on day 1. Both versions end with the same net
position. But fork-and-sync splits it differently between principal and excess,
because the copy it replays on keeps a stored running balance that already
includes the day 2 disbursal.

I was careful about what that does and doesn't show, and a reviewer made me more
careful still.

- **It is a statement about my model.** Calling it a property of the paper rests
  on a step-by-step mapping from the paper's pseudocode to the model, and that
  case also depends on two readings the paper leaves open. The mapping is
  published alongside the results.
- **One counterexample is mine, not the paper's.** It involves two events on the
  same date. My rules order those by id; the paper orders them by arrival. That
  case disagrees with *my* rule, and the paper never promised otherwise.
- **The other two cases show up in the real code.** Run through the project's own
  Haskell port and Java service, the same events give the same kind of
  difference, compared against those engines' own on-time results.

## Part 3: four events that don't finish

The paper's limitations section already warns that a missing exclusion in the
reversal replay *"would cause unbounded recursion"*. Measuring speed led me
straight into that area.

The two implementations handle a late event by replaying what followed it, and a
reversal by replaying what was recorded after the event it reverses. With a
reversal and two late events, those replays can contain each other. Four events
are enough:

1. a disbursal, dated day 6;
2. its reversal, dated day 9;
3. a disbursal recorded late, dated day 4;
4. a payment recorded late, dated day 2.

In the model, the replay never settles: it runs out of every recursion budget I
tried, from 8 to 512. Take away any one of the three ingredients and it finishes.
The Haskell port runs out of memory, and the Java service runs out of stack, its
two replay paths calling each other more than 200 times in the stack trace.

That is consistent with never finishing, but **it isn't a proof that it never
finishes**, and I've been careful to say so everywhere. It is also an edge case:
without reversals in the mix, fork-and-sync finished on every workload I gave it.

## Part 4: an optimisation that keeps its meaning

Finding what's wrong is half the job. The other half is a cheaper rebuild that is
*right*.

The idea is simple. When one event arrives late, or one is reversed, nothing
before it in date order changes. So keep those effects, and the running balance
at that point, and re-apply only what follows. The claim is that this gives
**exactly** the full rebuild's answer: the same balance and the same effect for
every event. It is stated in Lean for both a late arrival and a reversal, and
proved for both.

Before proving it, I tested it on 82,048 cases, and it never failed. Then I made
sure the tests could fail, by running plausible wrong versions on the same cases.
All six fail somewhere. The most instructive is the tempting shortcut for a
reversal: subtract the reversed event's own effect and leave everything else
alone. It is right most of the time and wrong in 5,004 of 27,120 cases, whenever
a later payment's allocation depended on the balance. "Re-apply what follows"
isn't caution. It is required.

## Part 5: how much faster

Then the speed, on the project's own Haskell port, unmodified. Three engines, the
same 32 workloads, and part 1's method: a discarded warm-up, three timed rounds in
rotated order, medians, and a strict rule for calling anything faster.

- Rebuilding from the affected point gave the full rebuild's answer on all 32
  workloads.
- It was faster on **all 32**: a median of **11.86×**, from 1.96× to 155.94×. The
  gain is largest when late events are recent, because less needs re-applying.
- Fork-and-sync's saving is real. Where it finishes, without reversals, it beat
  the full rebuild on 12 of 16 workloads. What it doesn't do is reliably give the
  same answer.

Two honest caveats. The project's ledger keeps entries in a plain list, so adding
one costs more as the ledger grows. Every engine pays that, and it shapes the
absolute numbers. And it's one laptop, one run. The measurements carry an expiry
date.

## Part 6: the reviews caught me too

The most useful thing this project produced may be the record of my own mistakes,
each caught by a review or by the checker:

- **A theorem that could never apply.** I proved the rebuild's balance
  independent of arrival order "assuming" a property that is false for events in
  general. True, and useless. The checker now insists on a witness that a proof's
  assumptions can hold.
- **A claim that couldn't fail.** My first "payments are conserved" statement held
  for any allocation at all, even one that paid seven times the amount to fees.
  Strengthened, it now rejects all three absurd allocations I tried.
- **A fix that broke something else.** My first fix for how adjustments are dated
  used events that hadn't arrived yet. An independent review spotted it. A test
  then counted 7,944 broken cases out of 119,100, against none before.
- **Words that said more than the evidence.** I wrote "follows the paper
  literally" when it also depended on readings the paper leaves open. Corrected,
  in public.

## What I'd take from it

- **Write the rules before reading the code.** If the rules come from the code,
  they can't disagree with it.
- **A counterexample is a gift.** One short, concrete case is worth more than a
  long argument, to the author as much as to anyone.
- **Check the assumptions, not just the proof.** A proof with an impossible
  premise is true and says nothing.
- **Make the tests fail on purpose.** If a test can't fail, it isn't protecting
  anything.
- **Have someone else review it.** Each review found something the proofs didn't.

## Limits

The model covers fixed amounts in one currency, with no interest yet. The proofs
are about the model, and connecting them to the paper rests on a published
mapping and two readings. The four-event case is not proved to run forever. The
speed figures come from one machine and expire. And the paper is a draft: some of
this may already be different by the time you read it.
