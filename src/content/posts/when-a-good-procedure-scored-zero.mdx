Arqon, my SQL Server analysis tool, gives every stored procedure an architecture
score: performance, risk, complexity, maintainability. Then someone ran it on a
genuinely good production procedure (set-based, filtering early, using an
indexed `NOT EXISTS`) and the performance score came back **0**.

They were right to be annoyed. A score of zero on well-written SQL doesn't just
look wrong. It teaches people to ignore the score, and a metric nobody trusts is
worse than no metric at all.

## The original formula

The first version was as simple as it gets:

```
performance = 100 - sum(per-finding penalty)
penalties: High -30, Warning -12, Info -4
```

Every detector that fired took a fixed bite. It was deterministic, easy to test,
and easy to explain. It also had two flaws I hadn't thought through.

**Flaw 1: penalties scaled with size, not with badness.** Several detectors fire
once *per occurrence*: a non-sargable predicate, a correlated subquery, a
leading-wildcard `LIKE`, repeated access to the same table. A large but perfectly
reasonable procedure trips the same warning many times, and nine warnings at -12
each is already past zero. The formula was measuring *length*.

**Flaw 2: "sometimes expensive" cost the same as "definitely broken."** A cursor
or an `UPDATE` with no `WHERE` clause is a problem, full stop. A non-sargable
predicate *might* be a problem, depending on the data, the indexes, and the plan.
The old formula couldn't tell the difference.

## The fix: confidence, per-code caps, diminishing returns

Every finding now carries a **confidence**, and it's a fixed property of the
detector rule, not a judgment from an AI, and not a guess at runtime:

- **High**: an unconditional problem, such as a cursor or an `UPDATE`/`DELETE` with no `WHERE`
- **Medium**: a strong signal whose real cost depends on data, indexes, or the
  plan, like a scalar UDF, a non-sargable predicate, `SELECT *`, a `WHILE` loop
- **Low**: a heuristic that's often benign, like repeated table access or dirty reads

Then the penalty is computed per finding *code*, not per occurrence:

```
basePenalty      = { High: 25, Warning: 10, Info: 4 }      by severity
confidenceMul    = { High: 1.0, Medium: 0.6, Low: 0.35 }   by confidence
occurrenceFactor = min(1 + 0.3 * (count - 1), 2.0)         repeats add at most +100%

perCodePenalty   = round(basePenalty * confidenceMul * occurrenceFactor)
score            = clamp(100 - min(sum of perCodePenalty, 100), 0, 100)
```

Two properties fall out of that:

1. **The same warning repeated can never cost more than twice a single one.** Ten
   non-sargable predicates are worth investigating, but they're one kind of
   problem, not ten.
2. **A low-confidence heuristic costs about a third of a high-confidence one.**

The score can still reach zero, but only through several *distinct*,
high-severity, high-confidence problems. Which is what zero should mean.

## A worked example

Take an illustrative procedure that trips one Medium-confidence warning six times,
and one Low-confidence warning three times.

**Old formula:** nine warnings × 12 = 108 points. Score: **0**.

**New formula:**

- Medium warning × 6: `10 × 0.6 × min(1 + 0.3 × 5, 2.0)` = `10 × 0.6 × 2.0` = **12**
- Low warning × 3: `10 × 0.35 × min(1 + 0.3 × 2, 2.0)` = `10 × 0.35 × 1.6` = 5.6 → **6**

Penalty 18. Score: **82**: a procedure worth a look, which is the truth.

## The part that mattered most: showing the work

The formula fixed the number. What fixed the *trust* was a breakdown. The scorer
now emits a contribution for every code (which rule, how many times it fired, its
severity and confidence, and exactly how many points it cost), and the UI shows it
under **"Why this score"**. Each finding also carries a chip saying whether it's a
*potential*, *likely*, or *confirmed* concern.

A score you can audit is a score you can argue with. One you can't audit, you
either blindly obey or ignore. And people ignore it.

## What I rejected

- **A hard floor ("never below 30").** Simple, but arbitrary, and it would hide
  genuinely terrible procedures behind a fake minimum.
- **Per-occurrence penalties with only a global cap.** One noisy rule could still
  dominate everything else. Capping per code is fairer across rule types.
- **Dropping numbers for confidence buckets.** The feedback praised the numbers for
  complexity and maintainability. Keep the numbers; add the honesty alongside them.

## What I took from it

- **A metric is a product feature with users,** and its users will find the case
  you didn't. That report was the most useful thing that happened to the scorer.
- **Deterministic doesn't mean correct.** The old formula was perfectly
  reproducible. It reproduced the wrong answer every time. Determinism makes
  a bug *findable*; it doesn't prevent it.
- **Confidence belongs in the data.** Putting it on the rule, rather than
  inferring it later, kept the whole system explainable and testable.

The natural next step is to let a captured execution plan promote or demote a
finding's confidence, since the plan knows whether that predicate really caused a scan.
That one is deliberately deferred until it can be done just as deterministically.
